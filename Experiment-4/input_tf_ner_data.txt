Natural language processing has a long history that begins well before modern computers. In 1950, Alan Turing published a paper in the journal Mind that proposed what is now called the Turing test, a way to judge whether a machine can hold a conversation that is indistinguishable from a human one. A few years later, in 1957, Noam Chomsky of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology published Syntactic Structures, a book that changed how linguists thought about grammar. Early machine translation research received generous funding in the United States until 1966, when the ALPAC report concluded that progress was too slow and the money largely disappeared.

Interest returned in the 1980s, when researchers at IBM built statistical translation systems trained on millions of sentences taken from the parliament of Canada, which publishes its proceedings in both English and French. Statistical methods dominated the field for the next two decades, and language modeling became a matter of counting words and estimating probabilities from large collections of text.

Modern deep learning then transformed the field again. In June 2017, researchers at Google published the paper Attention Is All You Need, which introduced the transformer architecture that now sits underneath almost every large language model. OpenAI, a laboratory based in San Francisco, released GPT-2 in February 2019 and GPT-3 the following year, and Microsoft invested one billion dollars in OpenAI in July 2019. Universities such as Stanford University and Carnegie Mellon University continue to teach natural language processing to thousands of students every year.

Today the applications of natural language processing are everywhere. Search engines answer questions written in plain English, phones transcribe speech in real time, and translation services such as Google Translate handle more than one hundred languages. The field keeps growing, and language remains one of the hardest problems in artificial intelligence.
